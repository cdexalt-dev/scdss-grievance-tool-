Need help from Suffolk County DSS because of a disability?

You can ask DSS for help or a change because of a disability. This can involve housing, food, health, benefits, appointments, communication, rules about work, or other DSS services.

If DSS says no, does not help, or gives you something that does not work, you can make a complaint. If your benefits or shelter assistance are also affected, you may need a State Fair Hearing, too.

What kind of help can I ask for?

These are common areas where a disability may make DSS services harder to use. Choose a topic to see more examples. You can also ask for something not listed.

Housing and shelter

Where you stay, who stays with you, and whether the space meets your needs.

  • A room to yourself, when sharing is hard or unsafe because of your disability
  • A room with a caregiver or support person
  • A shower, bathroom, entrance, or room you can safely use
  • A different shelter or room that meets your needs
  • Space and access for a service animal or medical equipment
Health conditions, food, and allergies

Help needed to safely manage a disability or health condition in DSS services.

  • Safe meals for a serious nut or seafood allergy
  • Food or meal timing that meets disability-related diabetes needs
  • Safe food preparation, storage, or another workable meal option
  • Access to medicine, electricity for medical equipment, or health care
  • Changes because of smoke, mold, temperature, or other health barriers
Benefits and money

Getting or keeping the help you qualify for.

  • SNAP (food benefits), Medicaid (health coverage), Temporary Assistance (money or emergency help)
  • Help applying for or keeping benefits
  • Asking DSS to fix benefits it cut or stopped after you did not get needed help
  • More time to turn in papers if DSS took too long to answer your request for disability-related help
Applications, appointments, and work

Changes to DSS rules or steps that are hard for you because of your disability.

  • Help filling out forms or getting paperwork
  • Rescheduled appointments, shorter waits, or fewer visits
  • Phone appointments or visits to your home, when needed because of your disability
  • Changes to work hours, job tasks, or work rules
Communication and access

Getting information in a way you can use.

  • Large print, Braille, audio, or digital files you can read or listen to
  • Sign-language interpreting or other communication help
  • Help reading DSS letters or talking on the phone
  • DSS offices and websites that you can use
People who can help you

Support with making requests or using services.

  • A caregiver or someone you trust who can speak for you
  • Help from DSS staff to ask for disability-related help or make a complaint
  • Help when a shelter or outside company provides DSS services
When DSS says no or the help fails

What happens if your needs are not met.

  • DSS says no, promises help but does not give it, or takes too long
  • DSS offers different help, but it still does not work for you
  • DSS cuts or stops your benefits, or closes your case, after not giving you needed help
  • Making a complaint or asking for a Fair Hearing

These are examples, not a complete list. DSS must look at what each person needs. No one is automatically promised a particular room, meal, or benefit just because it appears here.

What does “reasonable accommodation” mean?

It means help or a change you need because of a disability so you can use DSS programs and services.

Examples: Someone with a serious peanut allergy may need shelter meals prepared without peanuts touching their food. Someone with diabetes may need a meal arrangement that works with their health needs. Someone who needs help from a caregiver may need a different room arrangement.

DSS must look at the person's needs. The same choice will not work for everyone.

Where does this rule come from?

Newkirk settlement, sections II(A)(22–24) and VII(A), (D); Procedure 899, pages 5–6 and 9. The allergy and diabetes situations above are examples, not promises of a specific meal.

Know Your Rights

Your rights in four topics

DSS has rules for helping people with disabilities. These rules are called Procedure 899. Open a topic below to learn more.

A reasonable accommodation means help or a change you need because of your disability. A grievance means an official complaint.

1Getting help from DSSWhat you can ask for and what DSS must do

1. You can ask in your own words

Tell any DSS worker what help you need because of your disability. You can ask in person, by phone, or in writing. You do not need a special form or legal words. DSS must keep a record of your request.

Where does this rule come from?

Settlement, sections II(A)(24), VII(A)–(B); Procedure 899, pages 7–9.

2. DSS must work with you

DSS must look at your own needs and listen to what you think will help. If DSS cannot give you the help you requested, it must consider another option that actually works for you.

Where does this rule come from?

Settlement, section VII(A)(6), VII(D); Procedure 899, page 9.

3. DSS must act and tell you what is happening

For regular requests, DSS should usually decide within five workdays after it has what it needs, when possible. Emergency help may need a same-day decision. If DSS needs more time, it must explain the delay and the next steps.

Where does this rule come from?

Settlement, section VII(E)(1–5), including notice and emergency rules; Procedure 899, pages 9–11.

4. Your privacy matters

DSS should not ask for more medical details than it needs to work out your request. Information about your disability must be protected. Staff should also notice when someone may need help asking.

Where does this rule come from?

Settlement, section VII(C)(1–11); Procedure 899, pages 2, 8–9.

2What kind of help is possible?Housing, health, food, benefits, and more

5. Help can cover many parts of DSS services

Help because of a disability can involve shelter, benefits, appointments, work rules, communication, and other DSS services. DSS must also address these needs when outside companies or shelters provide services for DSS.

Where does this rule come from?

Settlement, sections VII(A)(1–5), VII(I); Procedure 899, pages 1–2 and 5–6.

6. See examples of help people can ask for

Here are possible requests. The list is not complete. DSS must consider your needs before deciding.

Housing, rooms, and shelter
  • A room to yourself if sharing is hard or unsafe because of your disability
  • Staying in a shelter with your caregiver or someone who helps care for you
  • A shower with grab bars, a bathroom you can use, or a room without stairs
  • A different shelter or room when the one DSS gives you does not work
  • Access for service animals and necessary medical equipment
Health conditions, food, and allergies
  • Food that is safe for a serious nut or seafood allergy
  • Meal choices or timing that meet disability-related diabetes needs
  • Safe food preparation, storage, or another food arrangement
  • Changes for medical equipment, medication, smoke, heat, or other health needs
Benefits, forms, and appointments
  • Help filling out or renewing applications and gathering papers
  • More time, a different appointment, or a phone or home visit
  • Changes to work tasks or schedules when needed because of a disability
  • Help when DSS cut or stopped benefits after you did not get the help you needed
Communication, reading, and support
  • Large print, Braille, audio, or digital files you can read or listen to
  • Sign-language interpreters or help reading letters
  • Ways to contact DSS or use its website that work for you
  • Help from DSS staff or someone you trust to speak for you

These are examples of help you can ask for. DSS must look at your needs before deciding. The examples do not promise you a particular room, meal, or service.

Where do these examples come from?

Procedure 899, pages 5–6, 17–20, 23; Settlement, sections VII(A), (D), (I); Newkirk comprehensive digest, page 20 (examples described as plaintiffs' allegations).

3When the help does not workDelays, denied requests, and benefit cuts

7. A yes on paper is not enough

If DSS approves help but you never receive it, or the help does not work for your disability, you can report that problem. DSS must track its decisions and when approved help was actually provided.

Where does this rule come from?

Settlement, sections VII(E)–(F); October 2025 SCDSS grievance notice.

8. DSS cannot just send you somewhere else

DSS may suggest another agency or service. But sending you somewhere else does not replace help DSS itself is required to give you.

Where does this rule come from?

Procedure 899, page 19, section 12.

9. DSS must look at why you could not follow a rule

If you missed an appointment or deadline because you did not get the help you needed, DSS must consider that before cutting or stopping your benefits. If DSS finds the unmet need caused the problem, it must undo a cut, stop, or restriction in benefits. A cut for not following a rule is sometimes called a sanction.

Where does this rule come from?

Settlement, section VII(E)(6–9); Procedure 899, pages 16–17.

4Complaints and next stepsHow to speak up and get an answer

10. You can make a complaint

If DSS says no to help you need because of your disability, or the help does not work, you can make a complaint (grievance). You can also complain if DSS treats you unfairly because of your disability. DSS must answer within certain time limits. If you disagree with the answer, you can ask DSS to review it again (appeal).

Where does this rule come from?

Settlement, section IX; Procedure 899, pages 14–16; October 2025 grievance notice.

11. You can get help making a complaint

DSS staff must help you file if you need it. Ask for another way to file if you cannot use a written form. Someone you trust may also be able to make the complaint for you.

Where does this rule come from?

Settlement, section IX(D)–(E); Procedure 899, page 14.

12. You still have other rights

DSS cannot treat you badly for making a complaint. You can also ask for a Fair Hearing, where New York State reviews a decision DSS made about your benefits or services—or a time DSS failed to act.

Where does this rule come from?

Settlement, section IX(G); Procedure 899, page 15; ADA 1 complaint form.

Do I need a Fair Hearing, a complaint, or both?

You do not have to wait for DSS to answer your complaint before asking for a Fair Hearing. These are two different ways to address a problem. You may be able to use both at the same time.

Fair Hearing

Ask New York State to review what DSS decided about your benefits or services, or a time DSS failed to act.

For example: DSS cut your SNAP benefits or denied temporary housing help.

Complaint (grievance)

Tell Suffolk County DSS that it did not properly handle help you needed because of your disability.

For example: DSS approved a disability-related change but never provided it.

When might I need both? When a disability-related problem also affects your benefits or shelter assistance. You can file a complaint and request a Fair Hearing without waiting for either one to finish.

Some examples

DSS says no to the help you need because of a disability.

Consider: Complaint.

If DSS has not also made a benefits or services decision you can challenge, the complaint process may be the better place to start.

You miss an appointment because DSS did not give you needed disability-related help. Then DSS stops your cash assistance.

Consider: Both.

File a complaint about the help you did not get. Request a Fair Hearing promptly to challenge the benefits decision.

DSS refuses to help you get shelter, or gives you shelter that does not meet your needs because of your disability.

Consider: Both.

A Fair Hearing may let you challenge a shelter-help decision or a time DSS failed to act. A complaint lets you report how DSS handled your disability-related needs.

Your shelter agreed to provide safe meals because of an allergy, but the meals still are not safe. No benefits decision has been made.

Consider: Complaint.

Explain why the meals are not safe for you. If you are also challenging a DSS shelter decision or its failure to act, ask right away whether you can request a Fair Hearing.

DSS has not acted on your benefits application when it should.

Consider: Fair Hearing.

You can also make a complaint if DSS did not give you help you needed because of your disability, and that caused the delay.

When should I ask? As soon as you know there may be a problem with benefits or services. You do not need to finish a DSS complaint first. Even if you already filed a complaint and are waiting for an answer, you can request a Fair Hearing.

How much time do I have? Fair Hearing deadlines are often 60 days for Temporary Assistance and 90 days for SNAP. Other programs or issues can have different rules. Check any letter DSS sent you (called a notice), and ask promptly.

Can my benefits stay the same while I wait? Sometimes. If DSS plans to cut or stop benefits, requesting a Fair Hearing before the change takes effect may allow your benefits to continue for now. The rules vary, and you might have to repay some benefits if you lose.

How do I ask? Visit the New York State Fair Hearings page or call 1-800-342-3334. You can explain your situation and have someone help you. The State—not this website—handles Fair Hearing requests.

Do not miss your deadline. Making a DSS complaint does not give you more time to ask for a Fair Hearing. This website writes a DSS complaint; it does not request a Fair Hearing for you.

Where does this information come from?

Newkirk settlement § IX(G) preserves Fair Hearing rights. See also SCDSS Procedure 899 pp. 14–16; New York State OTDA's Fair Hearing guidance and 18 NYCRR § 358-3.5. Availability, deadlines, and whether benefits can continue depend on the program and the issue.

After you file

What happens after you complain?

Here is what Procedure 899 says DSS should do after getting your complaint.

A workday is usually Monday through Friday, not including holidays. Calendar days include weekends.

Most complaints

  1. Send it as soon as you can. Usually, you must complain within 180 days of what happened.
  2. DSS confirms it got your complaint. Within 10 workdays.
  3. DSS reviews it. DSS prepares a written decision within 60 days of getting your complaint.
  4. You can appeal. If you disagree, you usually have 30 calendar days after getting the written decision to appeal.
  5. DSS answers your appeal. Within 30 calendar days of getting it.
Where does this rule come from?

Procedure 899, pages 14–15, section 8.

When DSS denies emergency help

This faster process is for someone whose accommodation needed for emergency or immediate help was denied. Not every urgent problem follows this process.

  1. File within 24 hours after DSS denies the request.
  2. DSS should confirm receipt within 48 hours, when possible.
  3. DSS should decide as soon as possible, or within 7 workdays, when possible.
  4. If you disagree, appeal within 24 hours of the decision.
  5. DSS must send the final appeal decision within 7 workdays after getting the appeal.
Where does this rule come from?

Procedure 899, pages 15–16, section 8.

What happens after I send it?

Most complaints: DSS must tell you it got your complaint within 10 workdays. DSS prepares its decision within 60 days. If you disagree, you have 30 calendar days after getting the decision to ask for another review (appeal). DSS then has 30 calendar days to send its final decision.

If DSS said no to help needed for an emergency: The special complaint process says to file within 24 hours after DSS says no. DSS should tell you it got your complaint within 48 hours when possible and decide as soon as possible, or within seven workdays when possible. If you disagree, appeal within 24 hours of the decision. DSS must send the final answer within seven workdays after getting the appeal.

These are the rules for DSS complaints. They do not replace a State Fair Hearing. If your benefits may be cut or stopped, ask about a Fair Hearing right away. Its deadline is separate.
